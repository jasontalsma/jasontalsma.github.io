_The goal of this ongoing project is to explore low-cost mycelium biomaterial for function and fiction._

Designing with current plastics is a losing battle and biomaterials will be one answer. I chose to focus this project on textile waste and what role mycelium and this particular waste stream can play together. Globally, 92 million tons of textile waste is generated per year with 134 million tons per year expected by the end of 2030<sup>[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8257395/)</sup>. The initial scope includes other common waste materials to develop a broader understanding of the technology.

## Process

Blue oyster mushrooms were chosen for their voracious appetite, tendency to overpower competition, and preference for cellulose and lignin. Minimal clean room or still air box techniques were implemented. No tools were used beyond those found at home. Mushroom spawn can be easily [purchased online](https://farwestfungi.com/products/mushroom-spawn) and substrates at local stores/trash cans.

I chose a few different approaches in order to maximize testing. Most of the substrate is a blend of coconut coir (~%70), wheat bran (~%20), and used coffee grounds (~%10). No precise measurements were used to create this mix and I also made use of old oats, pasta, corn chips, etc. to test the feasibility of using homemade waste. I wanted to explore the following with this first round of experimentation:

- How hard is it to grow structural forms without professional equipment?
- How does textile shape/size/placement affect mycelial growth?
- How does mycelium react to different common waste materials?
- How do different mold shapes/materials affect growth?
- Can this material look and feel high quality?

The final step of the process is to dry the mycelium forms. Around ~30% mass reduction tends to ensure all moisture is removed from the form. This step kills the mycelium/mushrooms and stops its growth; stabilizing the final form and inhibiting further decomposition.

## Results

The resulting products from this round of testing showed incredible promise. Not only were almost all of the tested forms functionally sound, but also intriguingly beautiful. A few of the most relevant results include:

- The mycelium was easier to grow and work with than expected and shows realistic potential for non-industrial opportunities.
- Cotton textiles behaved very favorably to mycelial growth and easily provided a living surface for both mycelium and mushrooms.
- The time mycelium forms spent in their molds, with only one surface exposed to air, had a huge effect on the final surface finish.
- A thin cardboard exterior creates an extremely solid structure.

## Other Results and Discoveries

![Other Discovery](/images/img_9853.jpg)

Only one version of post-processing has been tested up until this point: impregnating a dried mycelial form with soy wax to improve durability. The wax had no problem coating the form and sinking into the material a bit. Beeswax is a common option for impregnating mycelium "leather".

A surprise discovery was the mycelium sheets created in between the ceramic weights I was using for one of the molds. The ~1mm thick sheets were easily removed and handled. The sheets are semi-transparent and were able to dry while maintaining their shape; they were very brittle when dried.

Oyster mushrooms attempting to fruit but starved for oxygen will produce mushroom forms shown below. These forms are mildly compressible and can be grown and dried without any human intervention past the "planting".

## Concl. & Next Steps

Testing has so far shown mycelium grown on common forms of waste has potential as a feasible small-scale structural material. The next goals for this project are:

- Eliminate odor from dried cotton forms.
- Minimize all substrate ingredients besides cotton waste.
- Begin honing in on useable forms.
- Create complex structures with pre-structured waste (i.e. cardboard, paper tubes).
- Explore "packing peanuts" further.
- Monitor all completed forms for longevity.
